# Buy cheap stats, skip the official feed

A season-long fantasy basketball startup can launch on licensed NBA stats for **roughly $500 to $1,500 a year**. It does not need Sportradar's official feed, and it should not build on scraped NBA.com data. The market splits into two tiers. Self-serve budget APIs publish prices: balldontlie at $0, $9.99 or $39.99 a month; API-Sports at about $10–$35 a month; Goalserve at $1,200–$1,500 a year. Enterprise vendors (Sportradar, Stats Perform, SportsDataIO commercial) quote privately, with reported starting points from about $500 to over $1,000 a month. The binding constraint is commercial-use rights, not price. SportsDataIO's $99–$149 self-serve tier is licensed for personal use only, and balldontlie and API-Sports publish no clear commercial terms. Latency matters less than it seems: season-long scoring needs minute-level updates at most, and handling stat corrections for 24–72 hours after a game matters more. Sportradar's exclusive NBA deal covers the league's *official* data, which betting operators need and fantasy apps do not. Independently collected box scores remain freely sellable, and US case law protects fantasy use of player names and stats. The real legal exposure is visual: headshots, team logos and NBA marks all require licenses that no budget stats feed includes. One caveat applies throughout. The research team could not open any vendor pricing page directly, so every price below comes from search-index snippets of the cited pages and must be confirmed before budgeting.

## Published prices run from $0 to $1,500 a year, then go dark

Only a handful of providers post self-serve NBA prices, and they are cheap. **balldontlie** sells three per-sport tiers: free at 5 requests a minute, ALL-STAR at **$9.99/month** (60 req/min, adding active players, injuries and the stats endpoint) and GOAT at **$39.99/month** (600 req/min, adding box scores, live box scores, lineups, play-by-play and odds) ([balldontlie](https://www.balldontlie.io/account/); [sportsapis.dev](https://sportsapis.dev/apis/balldontlie)). The free tier excludes stats and box scores, so it supports little beyond a demo. **API-Sports** gives 100 free requests a day, then paid tiers of 7,500, 75,000 and 150,000 a day at about **$15, $25 and $35/month** on its own site ([api-sports.io](https://api-sports.io/sports/nba)). Its RapidAPI listings show different numbers, such as $10–$20/month for API-Basketball ([RapidAPI](https://rapidapi.com/it/api-sports/api/api-basketball/pricing)), and those listings are separate products. **Goalserve** charges **$250 for one month or $1,500 for twelve** for NBA/NCAA live data ([Goalserve](https://www.goalserve.com/sport-data-feeds/nba-api/prices)). Its pages are the only ones that say outright the feeds are meant for "fantasy applications" ([Goalserve](https://goalserve.com/es/sport-data-feeds/NBA-api/samples)).

Above that tier, prices disappear behind sales calls. **SportsDataIO** (the commercial arm of FantasyData) sells a self-serve Discovery Lab at **$99/month or $599/year** for Fantasy, and $149/month or $899/year for Fantasy + Odds, but that data is delayed to the next day ([Discovery Lab](https://discoverylab.sportsdata.io/); [sportsapis.dev](https://sportsapis.dev/apis/sportsdata-io)). SportsDataIO states that Discovery Lab is "personal, non-commercial" only. Any public or revenue-generating product needs a commercial license ([SportsDataIO](https://sportsdata.io/help/data-rights-and-licensing-questions)), and third parties estimate that license at **$500–$1,000+/month** to start ([Highlightly](https://highlightly.net/blogs/best-nba-apis-in-2026)). **MySportsFeeds** is free for non-commercial developers, with plans "starting at $49 per month" for core non-live feeds. Commercial and live access are priced separately ([MySportsFeeds](https://www.mysportsfeeds.com/feed-pricing/)). **Sportradar** publishes no prices. Its self-serve option is a trial key with 1,000 calls over 30 days at 1 query per second, and that key is not licensed for production ([Sportradar](https://developer.sportradar.com/getting-started/docs/your-account)). Directory estimates for Sportradar start at about $1,250/month ([SportsAPI.com](https://sportsapi.com/api-directory/sportradar/)). A competitor's "$10,000+/month" figure refers to betting-odds feeds and carries obvious bias ([SharpAPI](https://sharpapi.io/compare/sportradar-alternative)). **Stats Perform** (Opta) sells a dedicated fantasy package with stats, projections, news and widgets for season-long and dynasty formats. It quotes by request and says it "works with many startups" ([Stats Perform](https://www.statsperform.com/faqs/stats-perform-faqs-pricing-licensing/); [Stats Perform Fantasy Content](https://www.statsperform.com/products/fantasy-content/)). Genius Sports focuses on betting and has no visible NBA fantasy product for startups.

One cheap path into enterprise data deserves a call. Sportradar's **Acceleradar** program has offered US startups up to **a year of free "Core" (non-real-time) data**. At its 2016 launch, eligibility required companies under a year old with no more than $500,000 in funding ([TCB Mag](https://tcbmag.com/sportradar-launches-aacceleradara-free-program-for-sports-tech-startups/); [FSGA](https://thefsga.org/sportradar-introduces-acceleradar)). The program still has a terms page ([Sportradar](https://developer.sportradar.com/sportradar-updates/page/acceleradar-terms-and-conditions)), but no one has confirmed its 2026 criteria.

## Season-long scoring rewards correction handling, not speed

The vendors that document latency describe speeds a season-long app does not need. SportsDataIO's real-time box scores run **15–20 seconds behind broadcast**, clients can poll every 3–5 seconds, and NBA season aggregates refresh every 10–15 minutes ([SportsDataIO](https://sportsdata.io/developers/content-update-frequency); [SportsDataIO](https://sportsdata.io/help/refresh-rates-feeds-and-timing)). Stats Perform claims sub-second live scoring ([Stats Perform](https://www.statsperform.com/faqs/stats-perform-faqs-fantasy/)). Sportradar reserves its low-latency Push feeds for paying "Realtime customers" as an add-on that the trial excludes ([Sportradar](https://developer.sportradar.com/basketball/docs/nba-ig-push)). TheSportsDB's premium livescores update every two minutes ([TheSportsDB](https://www.thesportsdb.com/docs_about)). balldontlie, API-Sports, Goalserve and MySportsFeeds publish no latency figures.

For 9-cat and roto managers checking matchups during a game, a one-to-two-minute delay is acceptable. The detail that decides whether final standings are correct is **stat corrections**. SportsDataIO advises re-pulling box scores for at least 24 hours after a game, ideally 48–72, plus a nightly sweep of the past three to four days ([SportsDataIO](https://sportsdata.io/help/sports-data-integration-guide)). Its resulting guide re-runs results every two hours for 72 hours ([SportsDataIO](https://sportsdata.io/help/resulting-guide)). No budget vendor documents a correction policy. A startup on a budget feed therefore has to build its own re-pull loop and its own user-facing correction rules, with ESPN's published policy as a reference point ([ESPN](https://support.espn.com/hc/en-us/articles/360000099732-Stat-Corrections)). On balldontlie, the rate limit is the practical latency ceiling. At 5 or 60 requests a minute, an app cannot poll a full slate's live box scores, which is effectively why the $39.99 GOAT tier exists.

Reliability evidence is thin everywhere. No provider in the research publishes an uptime figure or SLA. No independent Reddit or Hacker News reviews from 2024–2026 surfaced, and most "best NBA API" roundups are written by competing vendors. The anecdotes that do exist are mixed. One developer saw balldontlie stats calls take 10–30 seconds and needed caching ([SportIntel notes](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@roizenlabs/sportintel-mcp/blob/f8a45ddbbee2feb94796eb7a42efcac559a0b05b/docs/development/BALLDONTLIE_INTEGRATION_SUCCESS.md)). A 2016 HN commenter called Sportradar expensive and "occasionally wrong" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=11837102)). A 2026 build guide judges smaller providers "viable for MVPs" but behind tier-one vendors on depth, latency and reliability ([Kanopy Labs](https://kanopylabs.com/blog/how-to-build-a-fantasy-sports-app)). In this market, a paid tier buys accountability and support more than it buys proven uptime.

## Sportradar's exclusive deal locks up the official feed, not the box score

The earlier market-gap analysis listed Sportradar's exclusivity as a barrier to entry. A closer reading shrinks it considerably. From the 2023-24 season through **2030-31**, Sportradar is the **exclusive worldwide distributor of the NBA's official data** for the NBA, WNBA and G League, with expanded player-tracking rights. The NBA took a roughly 3% equity stake ([Sportradar IR](https://investors.sportradar.com/news-releases/news-release-details/nba-and-sportradar-announce-landmark-long-term-global); [SportBusiness](https://sportbusiness.com/news/sportradar-fanduel-extend-nba-data-agreement)). Reported deal values vary, from about $1B ([SportsPro](https://www.sportspro.com/news/betting/nba-sportradar-betting-data-streaming-equity-worth/)) to over $3B ([Gaming Today](https://www.gamingtoday.com/news/nba-sportradar-exclusive-data-rights-equity-deal/)). The deal gives Sportradar control of the league's own in-arena feed, its tracking data and the "official" label. It does not give Sportradar ownership of the facts of what happened in a game.

Those facts are public. In **NBA v. Motorola** (2d Cir. 1997), the NBA lost its attempt to stop a pager service from relaying scores and stats gathered from broadcasts. The court held that a company paying its own collection costs was not free-riding ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/105/841/598844/)). The "hot news" theory left open in that ruling was narrowed further in *Barclays v. Theflyonthewall* (2d Cir. 2011), which treated Motorola's test as dicta and found the claim preempted ([Wiley](https://www.wiley.law/alert-1978)). In practice, unofficial vendors such as SportsDataIO still publish full 2025-26 NBA coverage and sell fantasy plans ([SportsDataIO](https://sportsdata.io/nba-confirmed-coverage)), and the research found no case of the exclusivity being used against such vendors. The exclusivity matters for three groups: regulated betting operators, whose regulators often require official data; products that want optical tracking or the fastest feed; and anyone marketing itself as "official." A season-long fantasy app is in none of them. The full contract text is not public, so its precise carve-outs for fantasy and media remain unknown.

## Names and stats are free; faces and logos cost money

The legal line for a fantasy app falls between facts and imagery. On facts, the precedent is strong and directly on point. In **C.B.C. v. MLB Advanced Media** (8th Cir. 2007), the court held that the First Amendment protects using player names and statistics in fantasy games, because the information is public and the use implies no endorsement. The Supreme Court declined review in 2008 ([8th Cir. opinion](https://ecf.ca8.uscourts.gov/opndir/07/10/063357P.pdf); [Studicata](https://www.studicata.com/summaries/united-states-court-of-appeals-eighth-circuit/c-b-c-v-major-league-2007-7cbbfl)). *CBS Interactive v. NFLPA* (D. Minn. 2009) applied the same reasoning to fantasy football ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2009/05/cbs-interactive-inc-v-national-football-league-p__)). *Daniels v. FanDuel* (Ind. 2018) extended Indiana's newsworthiness exception to fantasy operators' use of names, pictures and stats ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/indiana/supreme-court/2018/18s-cq-134.html)). These rulings bind only their own circuits and states, and publicity statutes vary. Still, no court in the research ruled against fantasy use of names and stats.

Imagery is where rightsholders actually sue. In September 2024, the MLB players' union sued FanDuel, DraftKings, bet365 and Underdog for using player images without a license. FanDuel settled two months later ([Covers](https://www.covers.com/industry/mlbpa-files-lawsuit-against-fanduel-draftkings-sept-17-2024); [Covers](https://www.covers.com/industry/mlbpa-fanduel-settle-lawsuit-over-name-image-likeness-use-nov-16-2024)). For the NBA, three separate rights apply. Team logos and league marks run through NBA Properties, which negotiates case by case and has no public license, and one developer reported months without a reply ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/130430)). Group player likeness, meaning three or more players, now runs through the NBPA's **PLYRS UNTD**, which replaced THINK450 in June 2026 and manages collective NIL rights for more than 500 players ([Sportico](https://www.sportico.com/leagues/basketball/2026/nba-players-union-plyrs-untd-1234936539/); [NBPA](https://nbpa.com/news/plyrs-untd-nbpa-launches-new-commercial-brand-to-amplify-collective-player-influence)). The NBPA does not use OneTeam ([Sports Business Journal](https://www.sportsbusinessdaily.com/Journal/Issues/2019/11/25/Marketing-and-Sponsorship/OneTeam.aspx)). Photographs are copyrighted on their own terms. Sportradar sells headshots through a separate Images API, some of them under Getty terms ([Sportradar](https://developer.sportradar.com/images-and-editorials/reference)), and SportsDataIO sells them as a separate News & Images feed ([SportsDataIO](https://sportsdata.io/news-and-images)). Sportradar's own terms bar league logos and player imagery without permission ([Sportradar](https://sportradar.com/special-terms-conditions/)). Buying a stats feed therefore never covers visuals. No NBA fee schedule is public. The only benchmark found is a 2003 NFLPA fantasy deal at **7.5% of fantasy revenue** ([Willamette](https://digitalcollections.willamette.edu/bitstreams/7a56d8df-94d7-4acc-8005-035f5e612d0e/download)), which gives a rough direction at best. The safe default is initials avatars, team abbreviations and team colors with no logos. Hotlinking headshots from cdn.nba.com is unlicensed use of copyrighted photos.

### nba_api fails on contract terms and on engineering

Scraping NBA.com through `nba_api` gets the facts but breaks the rules for obtaining them. NBA.com's Terms of Use govern every NBA digital platform ([NBA.com](https://www.nba.com/terms)), and stats.nba.com is undocumented and unsupported. The research could not retrieve the verbatim scraping clause, so a lawyer should read it directly. The engineering problem is clearer than the legal one. Developers consistently report that requests from AWS, Azure, Heroku, GitHub Actions and Colab hang or time out ([nba_api #176](https://github.lx164.workers.dev/swar/nba_api/issues/176); [Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/1664241/http-timeout-in-azure-python-web-app)). The commonly cited cause is Akamai bot protection that silently drops datacenter connections ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/threads?id=gek0z)). The usual workaround is a residential proxy or a non-cloud relay. That workaround is deliberate circumvention, which strengthens a breach-of-contract claim, and it still leaves live scoring exposed to unannounced endpoint changes mid-season. Scraping NBA's own live feed is also the closest fit to the free-riding pattern Motorola warned about. The other free sources fail in the same way. Sports Reference says outright that "you should not create websites or tools based on data you scrape" from its sites ([Sports Reference](https://www.sports-reference.com/data_use.html)), and ESPN's hidden API is undocumented and can change without notice ([Zuplo](https://zuplo.com/learning-center/espn-hidden-api-guide)). The research found no public record of the NBA suing nba_api users, but that absence proves nothing. A $40-a-month licensed feed costs less than engineering around blocks.

## The recommended path runs $0, then ~$500–$1,500/year, then a negotiated contract

**Prototype (pre-launch, private testing).** Use balldontlie's free tier or the $9.99 ALL-STAR tier, or API-Sports' free 100 requests a day, to build schema, scoring engines and category math. SportsDataIO's free trial covers every endpoint, but it returns **scrambled** game stats shifted by 5–20% ([SportsDataIO](https://sportsdata.io/help/scrambled-data)). That makes it fine for testing integration and useless for checking accuracy. nba_api is acceptable here only for local, offline research such as backtesting categories, never as a production dependency. The previous report recommended starting as a companion layer on Yahoo and ESPN leagues. If the startup takes that route, Yahoo's Fantasy Sports API supplies league and roster data under its own terms and requires the attribution "Fantasy data provided by Yahoo Fantasy" ([Yahoo](https://sports.yahoo.com/developer/); [Yahoo API ToS](https://legal.yahoo.com/us/en/yahoo/terms/product-atos/fantasysportsapi/index.html)). It still needs a stats feed for its own analytics. Use no headshots or logos at this stage, and keep the UI on initials and team colors.

**Launch (public app, first paying users).** Move to a paid tier with a **written commercial-use grant**. balldontlie GOAT (about $480/year) and API-Sports (about $180–$420/year) are the cheapest paths with live box scores. Before launch, email each one and get a commercial-use confirmation in writing, because neither publishes terms. Goalserve, at **$1,200–$1,500/year**, costs more but is the cheapest vendor that explicitly markets to fantasy apps, which makes it the most defensible choice if the cheaper two won't commit in writing. At the same time, request quotes from MySportsFeeds commercial and SportsDataIO commercial, and apply to Acceleradar. If Acceleradar's free year of Core data is still offered, it beats every budget option on data quality and costs nothing. Build the 72-hour correction re-pull loop on day one, whichever vendor wins.

**Scale (thousands of leagues, premium tier, a second sport).** Switch to a negotiated contract with SportsDataIO, Stats Perform or Sportradar Core, without the Push add-on. Expect roughly **$6,000–$15,000+ a year**, extrapolated from the third-party monthly estimates. The deciding factor should be bundled content, not latency. Stats Perform and SportsDataIO include projections, which the dynasty and 9-cat audience values. Sportradar appears to offer no NBA fantasy projections, which would mean buying a second vendor or building projections in-house. A multi-sport plan (hockey, baseball) also favors these vendors over per-sport budget APIs. Add licensed headshots through the vendor's images product only once revenue justifies the line item. Approach PLYRS UNTD and NBA Properties only if the product needs logos, group likeness in marketing, or "official" branding, and budget for a royalty.

## Conclusion

The data question looked like the startup's hardest barrier, and it turns out to be one of its smallest. Sportradar's exclusivity and opaque enterprise pricing make the market look locked. In practice, the facts a season-long app scores on are public, cheap feeds carry them, and the law protects using them. The real costs sit elsewhere. One is contract hygiene: budget vendors whose commercial terms are unwritten. Another is correction logic that no budget vendor documents. The third is the visual layer of faces and logos, which every competitor shows and which is the one input that cannot be licensed cheaply. That shapes the product as well as the budget. A categories-first app for power users can make dense stat views, not player photos, its visual identity, and turn the licensing constraint into a design choice. The main unknowns are whether balldontlie and API-Sports will put commercial rights in writing and whether Acceleradar still runs. Both can be settled with a few emails before any engineering commitment.
